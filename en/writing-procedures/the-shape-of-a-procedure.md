Controlled procedures are easier to review, audit and trust when they all look the same. The shape
matters more than any individual page.

## The five sections

[d360]: # 'multi-level-list styles="decimal,lower-alpha"'

1. **Purpose and scope.** What this covers and — just as important — what it does not.
2. **Responsibilities.** Who does each part, by role.
3. **The procedure.** Numbered steps, each one an action with a visible result.
4. **Records.** What evidence this produces and where it is kept.
5. **Document control.** Owner, approver, review date.

[d360]: # "/multi-level-list"

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Section one is where most procedures fail**
>
> "What this does not cover" is the sentence that stops somebody applying the wrong procedure to a
> situation that looks similar. It takes a line to write and it is almost always missing.

## Document control

Every procedure here carries the same control block, which is a snippet rather than typed text:

[d360]: # 'snippet name="controlBlock"'

> [!TIP]
> **Make the control block a snippet on day one**
>
> It is the fastest way to prove reuse to a sceptical team: change one file, and two hundred
> procedures pick up the change in one reviewable commit.

## What belongs elsewhere

[d360]: # 'table border="all" striped="true"'

| If you are writing | It belongs in |
| :--- | :--- |
| Why the policy exists | A policy document, linked |
| Background on the equipment | A manual page, linked |
| A decision between two procedures | Its own page, linked from both |
| A one-off instruction for one shift | Not in a controlled document at all |

[d360]: # "/table"

[d360]: # "private-note"

> Worth agreeing internally whether the five-section shape is mandatory or a default. Mandatory is
> easier to audit and harder to adopt; a default that everybody quietly ignores is the worst of both.
